\section*{Overview of Major Improvements}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{\protect\numberline{}Overview of Major Improvements}
\label{sec:overview}

\textbf{Revised estimation and stronger counterfactual validation.}

We replaced the earlier implementation that fixed policy effects using historical averages with a joint rank-$1$ estimation procedure for policy and region effects. We then broadened the empirical validation program substantially. The revised manuscript now includes repeated holdout tests across regions and time periods, comparisons to higher-rank and simpler benchmark specifications, a finer 9-category policy partition, temporal-transfer tests across pandemic periods, and sensitivity analyses that quantify how much unmeasured confounding would be required to overturn the induced policy rankings.

\textbf{More actionable prescriptive output.}

The original submission documented frontier trade-offs but did not yet translate those trade-offs into operational guidance. The revision now adds two complementary prescriptive outputs. First, we develop a pooled region-blind state-action decision tree that summarizes the simulated policy map using only per-capita and per-GDP state variables. Second, we implement a rolling-horizon version of THEMIS that uses dated information together with a warm-start approximation in the first month, and compare it with the realized historical policies.

\textbf{Sharper interpretation of the policy menu and sensitivity of cost assumptions.}

We now make explicit that the seven policy categories are bundle-level interventions rather than additive combinations of separately estimated NPI effects, and we document the OxCGRT mapping that induces those bundles. We also add sensitivity analyses for nonlinear mental-health costs, within-block adherence decay, and the policy-optimization horizon, so that the paper is more transparent about which modeling choices do and do not materially affect the policy conclusions.

\textbf{Clearer framing of contribution and scope.}

The revision positions the core estimation problem as a partially observed low-rank counterfactual matrix problem and connects it more directly to the relevant literature. At the same time, the paper now states its scope more precisely: THEMIS is not a day-zero oracle for a completely novel pathogen, but it can be deployed as an online decision-support tool once limited early-wave data begin to accumulate. We also make the limitations around omitted channels, variants, and external validity more explicit.

Thank you very much for this feedback. We agree that the previous manuscript did not provide sufficient empirical support for the observational counterfactual analysis. In response, we added a substantial set of new experiments aimed directly at each of the four endogeneity concerns you highlighted. We organize the additions in accordance with those four points:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{The assumption that policies have similar effects across different regions (policy-region interaction), which the authors acknowledge}
	
	We thank the Associate Editor for raising this point. We agree that the treatment of policy--region interaction is one of the key modeling choices in the paper, because the core counterfactual exercise requires us to infer the effect of policies that were not observed in a given region. In the revised manuscript, we have substantially strengthened both the exposition and the empirical support for this component of the model.

	\phantomsection\label{subsec:delphi}\label{app:separability}

	At a high level, our approach requires a structured way to relate policy effects across regions and interventions. For each region $R$ and intervention $i$, let $\gamma_{R,i}$ denote the reduction in infection rate associated with policy $i$ in region $R$. Because each region is observed under only a subset of interventions, the counterfactual exercise necessarily requires us to impute missing entries of the policy--region effect matrix. Our baseline specification assumes that
	\[
	\gamma_{R,i} = k_R \gamma_i + \epsilon_{R,i},
	\]
	where $k_R$ captures region-specific scaling, $\gamma_i$ captures the average effect of intervention $i$, and $\epsilon_{R,i}$ is an approximation error. Equivalently, if we define
	\[
	\Gamma := (\gamma_{R,i})_{R \in \mathcal{R},\; i \in \mathcal{I}},
	\]
	then our specification corresponds to a rank-1 approximation of $\Gamma$.
	
	In the revised manuscript, we now make this interpretation explicit. We also clarify that the policy effects are estimated from the data rather than imposed from historical averages. Specifically, while the previous version fixed $\gamma_i=\gamma_i^0$, with
	\[
	\gamma_i^0 = \frac{\sum_{R} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{D}_{R,i}} \gamma_R(t)}{\sum_{R} |\mathcal{D}_{R,i}|},
	\]
	the revised version uses $\gamma_i^0$ only as an initialization and estimates $\gamma_i$ jointly with the region-specific scaling terms. As before, for each observed region--policy pair, we define
	\[
	\gamma_{R,i} = \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{D}_{R,i}} \gamma_R(t)}{|\mathcal{D}_{R,i}|},
	\qquad i \in \mathcal{I}_R.
	\]
	
	To address the reviewer's concern directly, we now provide three complementary forms of evidence showing that the rank-1 restriction is a parsimonious and empirically well-supported approximation in our setting.
	
	\textit{1. Sensitivity to higher-rank specifications.}  
	The first revision evaluates whether allowing richer policy--region interaction materially improves the fit. For each $l \geq 1$, we consider the low-rank specification
	\[
	\Gamma = K^{(l)}(G^{(l)})^\top + E,
	\]
	where $K^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{R}| \times l}$, $G^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}| \times l}$, and $E$ is an error matrix. Entrywise, this becomes
	\[
	\gamma_{R,i} = \sum_{m=1}^l k^{(l)}_{R,m} g^{(l)}_{i,m} + \epsilon_{R,i}.
	\]
	When $l=1$, this reduces to our baseline specification $\gamma_{R,i}=k_R g_i$, where both $k_R$ and $g_i$ are estimated from the data via non-negative alternating least squares.
	
	In our setting, $|\mathcal{R}|=210$ and $|\mathcal{I}|=7$, so the maximum possible rank is $7$. Following your suggestion, we compare the models with $l=1,2,3$ using a holdout exercise. In each repetition, we randomly withhold $20\%$ of the observed entries of $\Gamma$, estimate the rank-$l$ model on the remaining entries, and evaluate predictive performance on the held-out set. The predicted value for a withheld entry is
	\[
	\hat{\gamma}_{R,i}^{(l)} = \sum_{m=1}^l \hat{k}^{(l)}_{R,m}\hat{g}^{(l)}_{i,m},
	\]
	and we compute
	\[
	\mathrm{RMSE}_l
	=
	\sqrt{
	\frac{1}{|\Omega_{\mathrm{test}}|}
	\sum_{(R,i)\in\Omega_{\mathrm{test}}}
	\left(
	\hat{\gamma}_{R,i}^{(l)} - \gamma_{R,i}
	\right)^2
	}.
	\]
	This exercise is conducted separately for three pandemic periods.

The rank-1 model attains the lowest holdout RMSE in Periods~1 and~2 and performs essentially identically to the higher-rank alternatives in Period~3. Thus, within the level of sparsity present in the data, the main interaction pattern is already captured by a single multiplicative factor.

	The counterfactual estimates are highly correlated across specifications and concentrated on the line $y=x$. These results show that the counterfactual policy effects used in the paper are not materially altered by moving from rank~1 to rank~2 or rank~3.
	
	\textit{2. Comparison with simpler alternative specifications.}  
	The second revision shows that the rank-1 structure is not merely parsimonious, but also meaningfully improves upon simpler one-dimensional alternatives. We compare our baseline model to two natural benchmarks:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Policy-only (homogeneous across regions):} $\gamma_{R,i} = \beta_i$, where $\beta_i$ is the mean of observed $\gamma_{R,i}$ values across all regions for policy $i$.
		\item \textbf{Region-only (homogeneous across policies):} $\gamma_{R,i} = \alpha_R$, where $\alpha_R$ is the mean of observed $\gamma_{R,i}$ values across all policies for region $R$.
	\end{enumerate}
	The policy-only specification ignores region-specific heterogeneity, while the region-only specification ignores policy-specific heterogeneity. Both are nested within the rank-1 model.

	These comparisons sharpen the interpretation of the rank-1 assumption. In the first two periods, the rank-1 model outperforms both benchmarks by a clear margin, indicating that both policy heterogeneity and region heterogeneity matter, and that their interaction is captured meaningfully by a single multiplicative factor. The region-only specification performs worst, which is consistent with substantial variation across NPIs. The policy-only specification performs better than the region-only specification, but still yields $6$--$16\%$ higher RMSE than the rank-1 model. In Period~3, all three models perform similarly, which is consistent with the much lower cross-region variation in policies during the later phase of the sample. Overall, these results show that the rank-1 model captures substantially more of the relevant structure than either simpler alternative, while remaining considerably more stable than higher-rank specifications.
	
	\textit{3. Connection to the low-rank counterfactual literature.}  
	The third revision is to position this assumption more clearly within the literature. Low-rank structure is widely used in counterfactual analysis for panel and observational settings with incomplete treatment support. For example, \citet{athey2021matrix} use matrix completion methods to estimate missing potential outcomes in panel data, \citet{bai2009panel} develop panel models with interactive fixed effects based on a low-rank factor structure, and \citet{moon2015linear} study linear regression models with low-rank cross-sectional dependence. Our use of a rank-1 structure is therefore not ad hoc. Rather, it is the most parsimonious member of a broader and well-established class of low-rank approximations for partially observed matrices.
	
	In summary, the revised manuscript now addresses this issue on three distinct margins. First, we show that allowing higher-rank interaction does not improve out-of-sample performance and does not materially change the counterfactual policy estimates of interest. Second, we show that the rank-1 specification substantially improves upon simpler models that ignore either policy heterogeneity or region heterogeneity. Third, we clarify that this modeling choice is consistent with a broader low-rank literature used for counterfactual analysis in related settings. For these reasons, we believe the revision now provides much stronger support for the use of the rank-1 structure as a parsimonious and empirically appropriate approximation for the policy counterfactual analysis carried out in the paper.

\item \textbf{Unaddressed potential confounding between NPIs and outcomes, as noted by the authors}
\phantomsection\label{app:confounding}
We thank the Associate Editor for raising this important concern. We agree that confounding is an inherent challenge in any observational study of NPI effectiveness. Because NPIs are not randomly assigned, changes in infection dynamics around policy adoption may also reflect other contemporaneous factors, including voluntary behavioral responses, seasonal variation, mobility changes, or shifts in testing intensity. In the revised manuscript, we therefore address this issue more directly through a new \emph{ranking stability analysis} that quantifies how much unmeasured confounding would be required to materially alter the policy rankings produced by THEMIS. The full analysis is presented in the new Appendix~\ref{app:confounding}; we summarize the main findings below.

At a high level, this analysis is designed to address the confounding concern at the level most relevant for the paper's conclusions. THEMIS is ultimately used to rank candidate policy sequences by total cost. Accordingly, the key practical question is not whether unmeasured confounding could change the absolute level of the estimated policy effects, but whether it could materially alter the \emph{relative ordering} of policies that drives the decision recommendations. The new analysis is intended to answer precisely that question.

\textit{Policy ranking stability under confounding perturbation.}  Following the logic of \citet{cinelli2020making}, we design a sensitivity analysis that directly targets the object most relevant for the paper's conclusions: the \emph{relative ordering} of candidate policies. Specifically, for each region $R$, we shrink the estimated policy-specific effects $\gamma_{R,i}$ toward their cross-policy mean
\[
\bar{\gamma}_R = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}} \gamma_{R,i}
\]
by a fraction $f \in [0,1]$, so that
\[
\gamma_{R,i}^{(f)} = (1-f)\,\gamma_{R,i} + f\,\bar{\gamma}_R.
\]
At $f=0$, we recover the original estimates. At $f=1$, all policies are assigned the same $\gamma$ within a region, so NPIs no longer have any differential effect. This perturbation therefore provides a transparent way to model the possibility that part of the observed policy contrast is driven by unmeasured confounding rather than true policy differences.

For each value of $f$, we re-simulate all $7^3 = 343$ hypothetical policy sequences through the full THEMIS pipeline and record the resulting cost ranking. We then compare the perturbed ranking to the baseline ranking at $f=0$ using two measures: the Spearman rank correlation coefficient $\rho$, and the top-$K$ overlap, defined as the fraction of the $K$ lowest-cost policies under the baseline ranking that remain in the top-$K$ set after perturbation. This construction focuses on the \emph{contrast} between policies, which is the quantity that drives the cost comparison in THEMIS, rather than the absolute level of $\gamma$, which is more directly affected by the overall transmission environment in a region.

Table \ref{tab:ranking_stability} reports the results for the four focal regions in the paper:

The results show strong stability across all four focal regions. In every region, the Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ remains above $0.80$ through $f = 0.90$, and $\rho$ never drops below $0.80$ before $f = 1.00$ (i.e., complete elimination of all differential NPI effects). At the midpoint $f = 0.50$, $\rho$ exceeds $0.99$ in all four regions. Spain is especially stable: $\rho$ remains above $0.98$ through $f = 0.90$.

In all four regions, the cost-minimizing policy at baseline is a moderate, time-limited intervention sequence (``Restrict Mass Gatherings and Schools and Others'' in the early months, tapering to a less restrictive policy). Where the identity of the optimal policy changes at small $f$ values (e.g., $f = 0.10$ for Germany and New York), it shifts to a closely related moderate sequence that differs only in the third month. The qualitative conclusion---that moderate, tapered interventions dominate both prolonged lockdown and no action---is preserved through $f = 0.90$ in every region.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the policy rankings produced by THEMIS are not being driven by particularly sensitive estimates of the differential NPI effects. One can eliminate 50\% or more of the estimated policy contrast without materially changing the ranking in any of the four focal regions.

We agree, of course, that no sensitivity analysis can fully eliminate the possibility of unmeasured confounding in an observational setting. At the same time, we believe this new analysis substantially strengthens the manuscript by showing that the policy rankings produced by THEMIS are stable to large attenuations in the estimated differential NPI effects. For this reason, while the revised manuscript does not eliminate the usual identification concerns of an observational study, it does show that the paper's substantive policy conclusions are robust to substantial weakening of the estimated differential NPI effects.

\item \textbf{The additive treatment of different NPI effects (highlighted by Reviewer 1)}

Thank you for raising this point. We agree that, in a setting with multiple overlapping non-pharmaceutical interventions, it is important to clarify exactly what object the model treats as a ``policy effect.'' In the revised manuscript, we now make this explicit. The THEMIS framework does \emph{not} assume that the effects of individual NPIs enter additively. Rather, each policy $i \in \mathcal{I}$ is defined as a mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive intervention bundle, and the corresponding quantity $\gamma_{R,i}$ captures the joint effect of that entire bundle in region $R$.

Specifically, the model is not written at the level of separate school-closure, gathering-restriction, and travel-restriction coefficients that are then summed. Instead, each day in each region is mapped to exactly one policy category, and the estimated infection-rate reduction factor for that category reflects the combined effect of all interventions active within that bundle. Thus, for example, the estimated effect for ``Restrict Mass Gatherings and Schools'' is the effect of that bundle as implemented in practice, not the sum of a standalone school-closure effect and a standalone mass-gathering effect. We apologize that this was not made clearer in the previous version.

To make this construction transparent, we now describe more carefully how the policy categories are built from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT) \cite{hale2021global}. The OxCGRT records multiple dimensions of government response, including school closures (C1), workplace closures (C2), cancellation of public events (C3), restrictions on gatherings (C4), closure of public transport (C5), stay-at-home requirements (C6), restrictions on internal movement (C7), and international travel controls (C8), each with a severity level and geographic scope flag. We binarize these indicators using the thresholds listed in Table~\ref{tab:oxford_mapping_r1}, and then aggregate them into two composite features:
\[
\text{Restrict Mass Gatherings} = \mathbf{1}[\text{C3} \lor \text{C4} \lor \text{C5}],
\qquad
\text{Others} = \mathbf{1}[\text{C2} \lor \text{C7} \lor \text{C8}].
\]
Using four building blocks---Schools (C1), Mass Gatherings, Others, and Stay-at-Home (C6)---we assign each region-day to exactly one of seven policy categories.

    \item \textbf{The assumption that policies maintain consistent effects across different seasons (policy-season interaction)}
    
    \phantomsection\label{app:time_transfer}
    We agree that demonstrating the temporal stability of the estimated policy effects is essential. If the $\gamma_{R,i}$ values estimated in one period do not produce accurate predictions when applied to a different period, the counterfactual analysis would be unreliable. We therefore follow the AE's suggestion and conduct an out-of-sample time-transfer test: we train the DELPHI model and estimate all $\gamma_{R,i}$ on one period, then evaluate prediction accuracy in a \emph{subsequent, non-overlapping} period using only the policy-driven counterfactual curve for $\gamma$.
    
    \textbf{Experimental design.} We train DELPHI on March 15--June 15, 2020 (Period~1) and evaluate predictions on June 15--September 15, 2020 (Period~2). For the evaluation period, we construct the THEMIS counterfactual $\gamma$ curve using the \emph{actual} policies implemented in each region during Period~2, combined with the $\gamma_{R,i}$ estimates from Period~1. Specifically, we define $\gamma_R^P(t)$ as a piecewise constant function of the region-specific reduction factors:
    \begin{equation}
    	\gamma_R^P(t)=\begin{cases}
    		\gamma_{R,i_0} & t_0\leq t<t_1\\
    		\gamma_{R,i_1} & t_1\leq t<t_2\\ 
    		\vdots & \vdots \\
    		\gamma_{R,i_{k-1}} & t_{k-1}\leq t<t_k\\ 
    	\end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    where $i_j$ is the NPI in effect in Region $R$ during time window $[t_j, t_{j+1})$ in Period~2. The question is whether this piecewise curve---constructed entirely from Period~1 estimates---retains enough incremental signal to improve, or at least not materially worsen, predictions relative to a frozen-$\gamma$ benchmark in Period~2.
    
    We compare THEMIS against four benchmarks: (i) \textbf{DELPHI}, the base epidemiological model re-fit on the training period data with its flexible, nonparametric $\gamma(t)$ curve; (ii) \textbf{DELPHI-constant}, which freezes $\gamma$ at its training-period endpoint value throughout evaluation (i.e., no policy adjustment); (iii) \textbf{DELPHI-lookahead}, a non-causal oracle that re-fits DELPHI on both training and evaluation periods jointly, serving as an upper bound on achievable accuracy; and (iv) \textbf{SIR} and \textbf{SEIRD}, standard compartmental models (susceptible--infected--recovered, and susceptible--exposed--infected--recovered--deceased, respectively) without any policy-adjustment mechanism. The SIR and SEIRD baselines are included to demonstrate that simple compartmental models cannot match THEMIS's accuracy. All DELPHI-family models share the same underlying dynamics; the only difference is how $\gamma(t)$ is specified during the evaluation period. We report the mean absolute percentage error (APE) of daily cumulative case predictions over the evaluation window. We use simulated annealing for global optimization to ensure robust parameter estimates across all models.

Four substantive insights follow. First, \emph{active epidemiological burden---not just cumulative tallies---drives the tree's key splits}. The root split is on cumulative economic cost, but the critical depth-$3$ and depth-$4$ splits that distinguish NPI~$6$ from NPI~$7$ and that separate exit from continued restriction are on \emph{active cases} and \emph{active hospitalisations} as a percentage of population. This reveals that the \emph{flow} of current infections, not merely the \emph{stock} of past damage, is the operationally relevant signal for policy selection.

Second, the exit-from-restriction trigger is sharp and driven by hospital capacity. Rule~R1 ($97\%$ pure, $n=546$) prescribes immediate release to NPI~$1$ when cumulative economic cost exceeds $16.9\%$ of GDP \emph{and} active hospitalisations have dropped below $0.019\%$ of population, for any $w \leq 0.71$. This is a concrete, monitorable trigger: once the hospital system is no longer under acute pressure, the marginal humanitarian benefit of continued restriction is outweighed by the compounding economic and mental-health costs. Rule~R3 ($87\%$, $n=90$) reveals that when hospitalisations remain elevated ($> 0.019\%$), a strongly economy-prioritising policymaker ($w \leq 0.24$) should \emph{still} release---the sunk economic damage is irreversible and further restriction merely compounds it.

Third, \emph{lockdown is optimal only as a preemptive, early-stage tool}. Rule~R5 ($60\%$, $n=624$) prescribes lockdown when economic cost is still low ($\leq 6.6\%$ of GDP), $w > 0.34$, and active cases have just begun to appear ($> 0.016\%$ of population). Lockdown never appears as optimal once cumulative economic cost crosses the $16.9\%$ threshold. Rule~R2 ($92\%$, $n=741$) confirms this: when economic cost is already in the $[6.6\%,\,16.9\%]$ range and active cases have surged past $0.27\%$ of population, the tree recommends NPI~$6$ rather than lockdown---the incremental epidemiological gain of lockdown over NPI~$6$ shrinks once mental-health damage has compounded, while its incremental economic cost continues to grow.

Fourth, Rule~R4 ($45\%$, $n=553$) is the most counter-intuitive leaf: even when $w \leq 0.34$ (economy-first), if cumulative deaths exceed $0.002\%$ of population while humanitarian costs remain below $55.5\%$ of GDP, the tree prescribes NPI~$6$. An economy-first policymaker might na\"{\i}vely prefer to relax restrictions, yet the tree has learned that the humanitarian tail risk from an unchecked wave eventually feeds back into economic damage (through health-system overload and mortality costs) that exceeds the savings from relaxation. This leaf operationalises the insight that \emph{early death signals should trigger precautionary restriction even for policymakers who primarily value the economy}.

\textbf{(B) Rolling-horizon NPI advisor with a warm start.} At each monthly decision date $t \in \{$Mar~15, Apr~15, May~15$\}$ we estimate the SEIRD parameters $\hat\theta_t$ from the publicly archived dated DELPHI snapshot fitted only on data up to $t$ (specifically, \texttt{Parameters\_Global\_20200413.csv}, \texttt{20200515.csv}, and \texttt{20200615.csv}), and we estimate the policy gammas $\hat\gamma_t$ from the cross-region observed gamma matrix on the window $[\text{start},t]$ via the rank-$1$ ALS imputation of \S3.2. When the policy window is too short for ALS (the very first decision), we use the factory's rank-$1$ gamma estimate as a warm start. The advisor evaluates each candidate next-month NPI $i \in \mathcal{I}$ by
\[
C_t^{\text{online}}(i) \;=\; \mathrm{TotalCost}\Bigl(\mathrm{THEMIS}\bigl(\pi_{1:t-1}, i, \underbrace{i,\ldots,i}_{\text{remaining months}};\, R, \hat\theta_t, \hat\gamma_t\bigr)\Bigr),
\]
recommends $\pi^\star_t = \arg\min_i C_t^{\text{online}}(i)$, and constructs the three-month sequence $\pi^{\text{online}}_R$ greedily across the three decision dates. We compare $\pi^{\text{online}}_R$ to the observed actual policy $\pi^{\text{actual}}_R$ and to the hindsight-optimal sequence $\pi^{\text{HS}}_R = \arg\min_{\pi \in \mathcal{I}^3} \mathrm{TotalCost}(\mathrm{THEMIS}(\pi; R, \hat\gamma^{\text{full}}))$, where $\hat\gamma^{\text{full}}$ is the rank-$1$ ALS gamma estimated on the full $[\text{start},\text{end}]$ window. Online and hindsight costs are evaluated under $\hat\gamma^{\text{full}}$ on a single ground truth so that the comparison isolates the policy-choice effect; the actual cost is the real observed cost computed via \texttt{policy\_type="actual"} in the THEMIS pipeline, identical to the actual-cost point reported in the main results section of the manuscript.

\textbf{(C) Revealed-preference analysis.} A natural follow-up to the equal-weight comparison is to ask: \emph{what humanitarian--economic trade-off weight was each policymaker implicitly targeting?} For each region, we define the \emph{revealed preference weight}
\[
w^\star_R \;=\; \arg\min_{w \in [0,1]} \;\frac{C^{\text{actual}}_R(w)}{C^\star_R(w)},
\qquad
C(w) \;=\; w \cdot \text{hum} + (1 - w) \cdot \text{econ},
\]
where $C^\star_R(w) = \min_{\pi \in \mathcal{I}^3} C_\pi(w)$ is the hindsight-optimal weighted cost. The ratio $C^{\text{actual}}(w) / C^\star(w)$ is the \emph{regret ratio}; $w^\star_R$ is the weight under which the actual policy looks closest to optimal. We then evaluate three policies---hindsight, online advisor, and actual---under this common $w^\star_R$, producing a ``fair'' comparison in which every policy is judged by the criterion the policymaker was implicitly optimising.

\rcomment{
	\begin{enumerate}
	\item No identification methods are used to separate out individual or combined impacts. Consequently, the piecewise $\gamma_R^P(t)$ may not reflect true causal responses to each intervention or their synergies.
	\item On any given day, 2–4 NPIs often co-occur. Averaging $\gamma_R(t)$ over these days attributes the joint reduction equally to each active policy, obscuring their marginal effects. Moreover, there may be interaction effects—either synergistic or antagonistic—between policies.

Thank you for raising this point. We agree that, in a setting with multiple overlapping non-pharmaceutical interventions, it is important to consider the interaction effects between different policies. We would like to clarify that the THEMIS framework does \emph{not} assume that the effects of individual NPIs enter additively. Rather, each policy $i \in \mathcal{I}$ is defined as a mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive intervention bundle, and the corresponding quantity $\gamma_{R,i}$ captures the joint effect of that entire bundle in region $R$.

Specifically, the model is not written at the level of separate school-closure, gathering-restriction, and travel-restriction coefficients that are then summed. Instead, each day in each region is mapped to exactly one policy category, and the estimated infection-rate reduction factor for that category reflects the combined effect of all interventions active within that bundle. Thus, for example, the estimated effect for ``Restrict Mass Gatherings and Schools'' is the effect of that bundle as implemented in practice, not the sum of a standalone school-closure effect and a standalone mass-gathering effect. We apologize that this was not made clearer in the previous version.

To make this construction transparent, we now describe more carefully how the policy categories are built from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT) \cite{hale2021global}. The OxCGRT records multiple dimensions of government response, including school closures (C1), workplace closures (C2), cancellation of public events (C3), restrictions on gatherings (C4), closure of public transport (C5), stay-at-home requirements (C6), restrictions on internal movement (C7), and international travel controls (C8), each with a severity level and geographic scope flag. We binarize these indicators using the thresholds listed in Table~\ref{tab:oxford_mapping}, and then aggregate them into two composite features:
\[
\text{Restrict Mass Gatherings} = \mathbf{1}[\text{C3} \lor \text{C4} \lor \text{C5}],
\qquad
\text{Others} = \mathbf{1}[\text{C2} \lor \text{C7} \lor \text{C8}].
\]
Using four building blocks---Schools (C1), Mass Gatherings, Others, and Stay-at-Home (C6)---we assign each region-day to exactly one of seven policy categories.

\textbf{Experimental design.} We train DELPHI on March 15--June 15, 2020 (Period~1) and evaluate predictions on June 15--September 15, 2020 (Period~2). \begin{equation}

		\gamma_{R,i_0} & t_0\leq t<t_1\\

Of course, because these parameters are estimated from observational data, we do not claim that the exercise by itself identifies causal policy effects under weak assumptions.

In lieu of this, we further provide a detailed sensitivity analysis to show that our conclusions are robust to any unobserved confounding. Specifically, following the logic of \citet{cinelli2020making}, we design a sensitivity analysis for the relative ordering of candidate policies. Specifically, for each region $R$, we shrink the estimated policy-specific effects $\gamma_{R,i}$ toward their cross-policy mean
\[
\bar{\gamma}_R = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}} \gamma_{R,i}
\]
by a fraction $f \in [0,1]$, so that
\[
\gamma_{R,i}^{(f)} = (1-f)\,\gamma_{R,i} + f\,\bar{\gamma}_R.
\]
At $f=0$, we recover the original estimates. At $f=1$, all policies are assigned the same $\gamma$ within a region, so NPIs no longer have any differential effect. This perturbation therefore provides a transparent way to model the possibility that part of the observed policy contrast is driven by unmeasured confounding rather than true policy differences.

For each value of $f$, we re-simulate all $7^3 = 343$ hypothetical policy sequences through the full THEMIS pipeline and record the resulting cost ranking. We then compare the perturbed ranking to the baseline ranking at $f=0$ using two measures: the Spearman rank correlation coefficient $\rho$, and the top-$K$ overlap, defined as the fraction of the $K$ lowest-cost policies under the baseline ranking that remain in the top-$K$ set after perturbation. This construction focuses on the \emph{contrast} between policies, which is the quantity that drives the cost comparison in THEMIS, rather than the absolute level of $\gamma$, which is more directly affected by the overall transmission environment in a region.

\textbf{1. A rolling-horizon version of THEMIS that can be deployed once early-wave data begin to accumulate.}

Your first concern asks directly: \emph{``could THEMIS be adapted to generate prospective forecasts mid-pandemic?''} We agree that the original submission did not establish this, and have therefore added a rolling-horizon version of the framework that uses dated information at each decision point, together with a warm-start approximation in the first month when the data window is too short for the date-restricted ALS estimator. The full development is given in our response to AE Comment 5 (item B, ``Rolling-horizon NPI advisor''); we summarize the key elements here.

At each monthly decision date $t\in\{$Mar~15, Apr~15, May~15$\}$, the advisor estimates the SEIRD parameters $\hat\theta_t$ from the publicly archived dated DELPHI snapshot fitted only on data up to $t$ (the actual archived files \texttt{Parameters\_Global\_20200413.csv}, \texttt{20200515.csv}, and \texttt{20200615.csv}, which were available to policymakers in real time), and estimates the policy effects $\hat\gamma_t$ from the cross-region observed gamma matrix on the window $[\text{start},t]$ via the rank-$1$ ALS imputation described in the revised DELPHI estimation section. When that cross-region estimator is not yet available at the first decision date, the procedure uses the factory's rank-$1$ gamma estimate as a warm start and then switches to the date-restricted estimator once sufficient data become available. The advisor evaluates each candidate next-month NPI under a one-step-lookahead rolling-horizon procedure and selects the cost-minimising policy, constructing a three-month sequence $\pi^{\text{online}}_R$ greedily across the three decision dates. We compare this to the actual implemented policy $\pi^{\text{actual}}_R$ and to the unattainable hindsight-optimal sequence $\pi^{\text{HS}}_R$ in Table~\ref{tab:rolling_advisor_v2} and Figure~\ref{fig:rolling_advisor_v2}.

The results are economically meaningful. In Brazil, Spain, and New York the advisor materially improves on the actual implemented policy: the gap from hindsight falls by $141.8$, $120.5$, and $117.3$ percentage points respectively. On average, the actual policy was $124.4\%$ above the hindsight optimum, while the rolling-horizon advisor was $39.5\%$ above it. We interpret this as evidence that, once limited early-wave data begin to accumulate, the framework can be used in a sequential decision-support mode rather than only for ex post evaluation.

We have also added a revealed-preference analysis (described in our response to AE Comment 5, item C; Figure~\ref{fig:revealed_preference}) that infers the implicit humanitarian--economic weight $w^\star_R$ each policymaker was targeting and compares all policies under that common criterion. The analysis confirms that the online advisor's gains over actual policy are largest precisely where actual policy was most over-restrictive relative to its own revealed objective, which sharpens the operational message: even granting the policymaker their own implicit value of life, the advisor would have substantially reduced weighted cost in three of four regions.

\textbf{2. A region-blind state-action decision tree that operationalises the framework's recommendations.}

Your first concern also asks: \emph{``If not, how would governments use the framework to inform decision-making?''} To address this, we have added a region-blind state-action decision tree (described in detail in our response to AE Comment 5, item A; Figure~\ref{fig:state_action_pooled_tree}, Table~\ref{tab:state_action_rules}, Table~\ref{tab:state_action_action_distribution}). The tree maps the observable pandemic state at the start of each month---together with the policymaker's humanitarian-vs-economic weight $w$---to the cost-optimal next-month NPI. Crucially, the state vector deliberately excludes any region indicator or region-specific covariate (population, GDP per capita), so the tree must learn rules that depend only on observable pandemic and policy history. We view the resulting tree as an interpretable compression of the simulated policy map for the focal regions, rather than as a separately validated predictor for arbitrary new regions.

Pooling $4{,}560$ (state, action) training points across four regions, three policy prefixes, and a 20-point weight grid, a depth-$4$ CART tree produces $16$ leaves with at least $20$ training points each. Four substantive insights follow that go well beyond ``early moderate restrictions'':

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Active epidemiological burden---not cumulative tallies---drives the key policy splits.} The depth-$3$ and depth-$4$ splits that distinguish lockdown from moderate restriction, and that separate exit from continued restriction, all operate on \emph{active} hospitalisations and \emph{active} cases as a percentage of population. The flow of current infections, not the stock of past damage, is the operationally relevant signal.
\item \emph{The exit-from-restriction trigger is sharp and quantifiable.} Rule~R1 ($97\%$ pure, $n=546$) prescribes immediate release to no measure once cumulative economic cost exceeds $16.9\%$ of GDP \emph{and} active hospitalisations have dropped below $0.019\%$ of population, for any $w \leq 0.71$. The rules are concrete, monitorable, and stated entirely in per-capita and per-GDP units. \textbf{3. The methodological contribution: a rank-$1$ framework for partial counterfactual matrices.}

We agree that the original manuscript did not articulate the methodological contribution clearly enough, and we have revised the paper to make that contribution explicit. The central methodological step underlying every counterfactual exercise is the joint estimation of policy and region effects under a rank-$1$ structure on the partially observed region--policy effect matrix $\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{R}|\times|\mathcal{I}|}$. We summarize that revision here.

The fundamental statistical problem THEMIS solves is one of partial-treatment counterfactual estimation: each region is observed under only a subset of the seven NPI categories ($24.9\%$ of $\Gamma$ entries are observed in Period~1, $19.9\%$ in Period~2, and $20.0\%$ in Period~3), and the counterfactual exercise requires reconstructing $\Gamma$ on its missing entries. The revised manuscript now positions this estimation problem squarely within the established low-rank counterfactual literature in econometrics and statistics. Specifically, \citet{athey2021matrix} use matrix completion methods to estimate missing potential outcomes in panel data with partial treatment support; \citet{bai2009panel} develops panel-data models with interactive fixed effects, which are a special case of a low-rank factor structure on a partially observed outcome matrix; and \citet{moon2015linear} study linear regression models with low-rank cross-sectional dependence. Our specification $\gamma_{R,i}=k_R\gamma_i$ is the rank-$1$ member of this class, with both $k_R$ and $\gamma_i$ estimated jointly from the data via non-negative alternating least squares. We believe this framing helps clarify the methodological core of the paper.

The empirical case for the rank-$1$ specification is now much stronger (full development in our response to AE Comment 1.1). Three pieces of evidence support it: (i) higher-rank specifications do not improve out-of-sample fit (Table~\ref{tab:low_rank}); (ii) the rank-$1$ model improves on simpler policy-only and region-only benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:alt_specs}); and (iii) the imputed counterfactual entries are highly correlated across rank choices (Figure~\ref{fig:rank1_vs_rank3_scatter}). Beyond COVID-19, the broader methodological lesson is that pandemic policy data exhibit the partial-support structure for which low-rank regularization is particularly natural: many regions, many candidate interventions, but only a few interventions per region.

While the authors acknowledge methodological limitations in rigor, their effort in analyzing extensive real-world data is commendable. We share the view that the right standard for this paper is its applied and empirical contribution rather than the derivation of a new structural theorem about optimal control of epidemics. There is a long-standing tradition in MSOM and the broader operations management literature of impact-driven applied work whose contribution lies not in a single new analytical result but in the careful integration, calibration, and deployment of a decision framework on a problem of major societal importance. THEMIS sits squarely in this tradition. Concretely, the contributions of the revised paper are: (i) a cross-region, cost-internalizing counterfactual evaluation of the full $7^3$ space of three-month NPI sequences, calibrated on data from $210$ regions and four focal countries; (ii) the explicit framing of the policy-effect imputation problem as a partially observed low-rank matrix problem, with the empirical validation of the rank-$1$ structure described above and in the new Appendix~\ref{app:separability}; (iii) a rolling-horizon advisor that demonstrates how the framework can be used sequentially once early-wave data begin to accumulate; (iv) a region-blind state-action tree that compresses the simulation output into monitorable thresholds stated in per-capita and per-GDP units; and (v) a fully open-source codebase that makes the framework reusable for other regions.

To answer your final question directly---\emph{``is there a novel insight about trade-offs, timing, or regional heterogeneity that generalizes across contexts?''}---we believe the answer is yes, but in a deliberately qualified sense. At the policy-rule level, the decision tree highlights a set of recurring patterns within the four focal regions: active rather than cumulative burden drives the key splits, exit is tied to hospital pressure and accumulated economic cost, and lockdown appears only as an early preemptive tool. At the framework level, the paper shows that a parsimonious rank-$1$ counterfactual structure can organize a large sparse policy matrix and deliver stable qualitative conclusions under a broad set of robustness checks. Thank you for raising this important point. We have now provided much stronger empirical support for the separability assumption $\gamma_{R,i}=k_R\gamma_i$. We assembled the observed $\gamma_{R,i}$ matrix from 210 regions across three distinct pandemic periods and compared rank-1, rank-2, and rank-3 models with non-negativity constraints (see Table \ref{tab:low_rank}).
