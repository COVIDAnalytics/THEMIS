\section*{Overview of Major Improvements}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{\protect\numberline{}Overview of Major Improvements}
\label{sec:overview}

\textbf{Revised estimation and stronger counterfactual validation.}

We updated the earlier implementation that fixed policy effects using historical averages with a joint rank-$1$ estimation procedure for policy and region effects. We then broadened the empirical validation program substantially. The revised manuscript now includes repeated holdout tests across regions and time periods, comparisons to higher-rank and simpler benchmark specifications, a finer 9-category policy partition, time-transfer validation across pandemic periods, and sensitivity analyses that quantify how much unmeasured confounding would be required to overturn the induced policy rankings.

\textbf{More actionable prescriptive output.}

The original submission documented frontier trade-offs but did not yet translate those trade-offs into operational guidance. The revision now adds two complementary prescriptive outputs. First, we develop a state-action decision tree that summarizes the optimal policy map. Second, we implement Online THEMIS as a sequential one-step-look-ahead decision procedure and show that it moves three of the four focal regions substantially closer to the hindsight frontier.

\textbf{Sharper interpretation of the policy menu and sensitivity of cost assumptions.}

We now make explicit that the seven policy categories are bundle-level interventions rather than additive combinations of separately estimated NPI effects, and we document the OxCGRT mapping that induces those bundles. We also add sensitivity analyses for nonlinear mental-health costs and within-block adherence decay. In this response, we further discuss the policy-optimization horizon so that the role of that modeling choice is transparent.

\textbf{Clearer framing of contribution and scope.}

The revision positions the core estimation problem as a partially observed low-rank counterfactual matrix problem and connects it more directly to the relevant literature. At the same time, the paper now states its scope more precisely: THEMIS is not a day-zero oracle for a completely novel pathogen, but it can be deployed as an online decision-support tool once limited early-wave data begin to accumulate. We also make the limitations around omitted channels, variants, and external validity more explicit.

Thank you very much for this feedback. We agree that the previous manuscript did not provide sufficient empirical support for the observational counterfactual analysis. In response, we added a substantial set of new experiments aimed directly at each of the four endogeneity concerns you highlighted. We organize the additions in accordance with those four points:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{The assumption that policies have similar effects across different regions (policy-region interaction), which the authors acknowledge}
	
	We thank the Associate Editor for raising this point. We agree that the treatment of policy--region interaction is one of the key modeling choices in the paper, because the core counterfactual exercise requires us to infer the effect of policies that were not observed in a given region. In the revised manuscript, we have substantially strengthened both the exposition and the empirical support for this component of the model.

	\phantomsection\label{subsec:delphi}\label{app:separability}

	At a high level, our approach requires a structured way to relate policy effects across regions and interventions. For each region $R$ and intervention $i$, let $\gamma_{R,i}$ denote the reduction in infection rate associated with policy $i$ in region $R$. Because each region is observed under only a subset of interventions, the counterfactual exercise necessarily requires us to impute missing entries of the policy--region effect matrix. Our baseline specification assumes that
	\[
	\gamma_{R,i} = k_R \gamma_i + \epsilon_{R,i},
	\]
	where $k_R$ captures region-specific scaling, $\gamma_i$ captures the average effect of intervention $i$, and $\epsilon_{R,i}$ is an approximation error. Equivalently, if we define
	\[
	\Gamma := (\gamma_{R,i})_{R \in \mathcal{R},\; i \in \mathcal{I}},
	\]
	then our specification corresponds to a rank-1 approximation of $\Gamma$.
	
	In the revised manuscript, we now make this interpretation explicit. We also clarify that the policy effects are estimated from the data rather than imposed from historical averages. Specifically, while the previous version fixed $\gamma_i=\gamma_i^0$, with
	\[
	\gamma_i^0 = \frac{\sum_{R} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{D}_{R,i}} \gamma_R(t)}{\sum_{R} |\mathcal{D}_{R,i}|},
	\]
	the revised version uses $\gamma_i^0$ only as an initialization and estimates $\gamma_i$ jointly with the region-specific scaling terms. As before, for each observed region--policy pair, we define
	\[
	\gamma_{R,i} = \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{D}_{R,i}} \gamma_R(t)}{|\mathcal{D}_{R,i}|},
	\qquad i \in \mathcal{I}_R.
	\]
	
	To address the reviewer's concern directly, we now provide three complementary forms of evidence showing that the rank-1 restriction is a parsimonious and empirically well-supported approximation in our setting.
	
	\textit{1. Sensitivity to higher-rank specifications.}  
	The first revision evaluates whether allowing richer policy--region interaction materially improves the fit. For each $l \geq 1$, we consider the low-rank specification
	\[
	\Gamma = K^{(l)}(G^{(l)})^\top + E,
	\]
	where $K^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{R}| \times l}$, $G^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}| \times l}$, and $E$ is an error matrix. Entrywise, this becomes
	\[
	\gamma_{R,i} = \sum_{m=1}^l k^{(l)}_{R,m} \gamma^{(l)}_{i,m} + \epsilon_{R,i}.
	\]
	When $l=1$, this reduces to our baseline specification $\gamma_{R,i}=k_R \gamma_i$, where both $k_R$ and $\gamma_i$ are estimated from the data via non-negative alternating least squares.
	
	In our setting, $|\mathcal{R}|=210$ and $|\mathcal{I}|=7$, so the maximum possible rank is $7$. Following your suggestion, we compare the models with $l=1,2,3$ using a holdout exercise. In each repetition, we randomly withhold $20\%$ of the observed entries of $\Gamma$, estimate the rank-$l$ model on the remaining entries, and evaluate predictive performance on the held-out set. The predicted value for a withheld entry is
	\[
	\hat{\gamma}_{R,i}^{(l)} = \sum_{m=1}^l \hat{k}^{(l)}_{R,m}\hat{\gamma}^{(l)}_{i,m},
	\]
	and we compute
	\[
	\mathrm{RMSE}_l
	=
	\sqrt{
	\frac{1}{|\Omega_{\mathrm{test}}|}
	\sum_{(R,i)\in\Omega_{\mathrm{test}}}
	\left(
	\hat{\gamma}_{R,i}^{(l)} - \gamma_{R,i}
	\right)^2
	}.
	\]
	This exercise is conducted separately for three pandemic periods.

	These results show that allowing additional latent factors does not improve out-of-sample performance. The rank-1 model attains the lowest holdout RMSE in Periods~1 and~2 and performs essentially identically to the higher-rank alternatives in Period~3. Thus, within the level of sparsity present in the data, the main interaction pattern is already captured by a single multiplicative factor.

	\begin{figure}[htbp]

	The counterfactual estimates are highly correlated across specifications and concentrated on the line $y=x$. These results show that the counterfactual policy effects used in the paper are not materially altered by moving from rank~1 to rank~2 or rank~3.
	
	\textit{2. Comparison with simpler alternative specifications.}  
	The second revision shows that the rank-1 structure is not merely parsimonious, but also meaningfully improves upon simpler one-dimensional alternatives. We compare our baseline model to two natural benchmarks:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Policy-only (homogeneous across regions):} $\gamma_{R,i} = \beta_i$, where $\beta_i$ is the mean of observed $\gamma_{R,i}$ values across all regions for policy $i$.
		\item \textbf{Region-only (homogeneous across policies):} $\gamma_{R,i} = \alpha_R$, where $\alpha_R$ is the mean of observed $\gamma_{R,i}$ values across all policies for region $R$.
	\end{enumerate}
	The policy-only specification ignores region-specific heterogeneity, while the region-only specification ignores policy-specific heterogeneity. Both are nested within the rank-1 model.

	These comparisons sharpen the interpretation of the rank-1 assumption. In the first two periods, the rank-1 model outperforms both benchmarks by a clear margin, indicating that both policy heterogeneity and region heterogeneity matter, and that their interaction is captured meaningfully by a single multiplicative factor. The region-only specification performs worst, which is consistent with substantial variation across NPIs. The policy-only specification performs better than the region-only specification, but still yields $6$--$16\%$ higher RMSE than the rank-1 model. In Period~3, all three models perform similarly, which is consistent with the much lower cross-region variation in policies during the later phase of the sample. Overall, these results show that the rank-1 model captures substantially more of the relevant structure than either simpler alternative, while remaining considerably more stable than higher-rank specifications.
	
	\textit{3. Connection to the low-rank counterfactual literature.}  
	The third revision is to position this assumption more clearly within the literature. Low-rank structure is widely used in counterfactual analysis for panel and observational settings with incomplete treatment support. For example, \citet{athey2021matrix} use matrix completion methods to estimate missing potential outcomes in panel data, \citet{bai2009panel} develop panel models with interactive fixed effects based on a low-rank factor structure, and \citet{moon2015linear} study linear regression models with low-rank cross-sectional dependence. Our use of a rank-1 structure is therefore not ad hoc. Rather, it is the most parsimonious member of a broader and well-established class of low-rank approximations for partially observed matrices.
	
	In summary, the revised manuscript now addresses this issue on three distinct margins. First, we show that allowing higher-rank interaction does not improve out-of-sample performance and does not materially change the counterfactual policy estimates of interest. Second, we show that the rank-1 specification substantially improves upon simpler models that ignore either policy heterogeneity or region heterogeneity. Third, we clarify that this modeling choice is consistent with a broader low-rank literature used for counterfactual analysis in related settings. For these reasons, we believe the revision now provides much stronger support for the use of the rank-1 structure as a parsimonious and empirically appropriate approximation for the policy counterfactual analysis carried out in the paper.

\item \textbf{Unaddressed potential confounding between NPIs and outcomes, as noted by the authors}
\phantomsection\label{app:confounding}
We agree that confounding is an inherent challenge in any observational study of NPI effectiveness. Because NPIs are not randomly assigned, changes in infection dynamics around policy adoption may also reflect other contemporaneous factors, including voluntary behavioral responses, seasonal variation, mobility changes, or shifts in testing intensity. In the revised manuscript, we therefore address this issue more directly through a new \emph{ranking stability analysis} that quantifies how much unmeasured confounding would be required to materially alter the policy rankings produced by THEMIS. The full analysis is presented in the new Appendix~\ref*{ms-app:confounding}; we summarize the main findings below.

At a high level, this analysis is designed to address the confounding concern at the level most relevant for the paper's conclusions. THEMIS is ultimately used to rank candidate policy sequences by total cost. Accordingly, the key practical question is not whether unmeasured confounding could change the absolute level of the estimated policy effects, but whether it could materially alter the \emph{relative ordering} of policies that drives the decision recommendations. The new analysis is intended to answer precisely that question.

\textit{Policy ranking stability under confounding perturbation.}  Following the logic of \citet{cinelli2020making}, we design a sensitivity analysis that directly targets the object most relevant for the paper's conclusions: the \emph{relative ordering} of candidate policies. Specifically, for each region $R$, we shrink the estimated policy-specific effects $\gamma_{R,i}$ toward their cross-policy mean
\[
\bar{\gamma}_R = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}} \gamma_{R,i}
\]
by a fraction $f \in [0,1]$, so that
\[
\gamma_{R,i}^{(f)} = (1-f)\,\gamma_{R,i} + f\,\bar{\gamma}_R.
\]
At $f=0$, we recover the original estimates. At $f=1$, all policies are assigned the same $\gamma$ within a region, so NPIs no longer have any differential effect. This perturbation therefore provides a transparent way to model the possibility that part of the observed policy contrast is driven by unmeasured confounding rather than true policy differences.

For each value of $f$, we re-simulate all $7^3 = 343$ hypothetical policy sequences through the full THEMIS pipeline and record the resulting cost ranking. We then compare the perturbed ranking to the baseline ranking at $f=0$ using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient $\rho$ and record the first perturbation level at which the cost-minimizing policy changes. This construction focuses on the \emph{contrast} between policies, which is the quantity that drives the cost comparison in THEMIS, rather than the absolute level of $\gamma$, which is more directly affected by the overall transmission environment in a region.

Table \ref{tab:ranking_stability} reports the results for the four focal regions in the paper:

	\centering

		\hline

The results show strong stability across all four focal regions. In every region, the Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ remains above $0.80$ through $f = 0.90$, and $\rho$ never drops below $0.80$ before $f = 1.00$ (i.e., complete elimination of all differential NPI effects). At the midpoint $f = 0.50$, $\rho$ exceeds $0.99$ in all four regions. Spain is especially stable: $\rho$ remains above $0.98$ through $f = 0.90$.

In all four regions, the cost-minimizing policy at baseline is a moderate, time-limited intervention sequence (``Restrict Mass Gatherings and Schools and Others'' in the early months, tapering to a less restrictive policy). Where the identity of the optimal policy changes at small $f$ values (e.g., $f = 0.10$ for Germany and New York), it shifts to a closely related moderate sequence that differs only in the third month. The qualitative conclusion, that moderate, tapered interventions dominate both prolonged lockdown and no action, is preserved through $f = 0.90$ in every region.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the policy rankings produced by THEMIS are not being driven by particularly sensitive estimates of the differential NPI effects. One can eliminate 50\% or more of the estimated policy contrast without materially changing the ranking in any of the four focal regions.

We agree, of course, that no sensitivity analysis can fully eliminate the possibility of unmeasured confounding in an observational setting. At the same time, we believe this new analysis substantially strengthens the manuscript by showing that the policy rankings produced by THEMIS are stable to large attenuations in the estimated differential NPI effects. For this reason, while the revised manuscript does not eliminate the usual identification concerns of an observational study, it does show that the paper's substantive policy conclusions are robust to substantial weakening of the estimated differential NPI effects.

\item \textbf{The additive treatment of different NPI effects (highlighted by Reviewer 1)}

Thank you for raising this point. We agree that, in a setting with multiple overlapping non-pharmaceutical interventions, it is important to clarify exactly what object the model treats as a ``policy effect.'' In the revised manuscript, we now make this explicit. The THEMIS framework does \emph{not} assume that the effects of individual NPIs enter additively. Rather, each policy $i \in \mathcal{I}$ is defined as a mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive intervention bundle, and the corresponding quantity $\gamma_{R,i}$ captures the joint effect of that entire bundle in region $R$.

Specifically, the model is not written at the level of separate school-closure, gathering-restriction, and travel-restriction coefficients that are then summed. Instead, each day in each region is mapped to exactly one policy category, and the estimated infection-rate reduction factor for that category reflects the combined effect of all interventions active within that bundle. Thus, for example, the estimated effect for ``Restrict Mass Gatherings and Schools'' is the effect of that bundle as implemented in practice, not the sum of a standalone school-closure effect and a standalone mass-gathering effect. We apologize that this was not made clearer in the previous version.

To make this construction transparent, we now describe more carefully how the policy categories are built from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT) \cite{hale2021global}. The OxCGRT records multiple dimensions of government response, including school closures (C1), workplace closures (C2), cancellation of public events (C3), restrictions on gatherings (C4), closure of public transport (C5), stay-at-home requirements (C6), restrictions on internal movement (C7), and international travel controls (C8), each with a severity level and geographic scope flag. We binarize these indicators using the thresholds listed in Table~\ref{tab:oxford_mapping_r1}, and then aggregate them into two composite features:
\[
\text{Restrict Mass Gatherings} = \mathbf{1}[\text{C3} \lor \text{C4} \lor \text{C5}],
\qquad
\text{Others} = \mathbf{1}[\text{C2} \lor \text{C7} \lor \text{C8}].
\]
Using four building blocks (Schools (C1), Mass Gatherings, Others, and Stay-at-Home (C6)), we assign each region-day to exactly one of seven policy categories.

    \item \textbf{The assumption that policy effects estimated in one period retain predictive content in later periods}
    
    \phantomsection\label{app:time_transfer}
    We agree that demonstrating the temporal stability of the estimated policy effects is essential. If the $\gamma_{R,i}$ values estimated in one period do not produce accurate predictions when applied to a different period, the counterfactual analysis would be unreliable. We therefore follow the AE's suggestion and conduct an out-of-sample time-transfer validation: we train the DELPHI model and estimate all $\gamma_{R,i}$ on one period, then evaluate prediction accuracy in a \emph{subsequent, non-overlapping} period using only the policy-driven counterfactual curve for $\gamma$.
    
    \textbf{Experimental design.} We train DELPHI on March 15--June 15, 2020 (Period~1) and evaluate predictions on June 15--September 15, 2020 (Period~2). For the evaluation period, we construct the THEMIS counterfactual $\gamma$ curve using the \emph{actual} policies implemented in each region during Period~2, combined with the $\gamma_{R,i}$ estimates from Period~1. Specifically, we define $\gamma_R^P(t)$ as a piecewise constant function of the region-specific reduction factors:
    \begin{equation}
    	\gamma_R^P(t)=\begin{cases}
    		\gamma_{R,i_0} & t_0\leq t<t_1\\
    		\gamma_{R,i_1} & t_1\leq t<t_2\\ 
    		\vdots & \vdots \\
    		\gamma_{R,i_{k-1}} & t_{k-1}\leq t<t_k\\ 
    	\end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    where $i_j$ is the NPI in effect in Region $R$ during time window $[t_j, t_{j+1})$ in Period~2. The question is whether this piecewise curve, constructed entirely from Period~1 estimates, retains enough incremental signal to improve, or at least not materially worsen, predictions relative to a frozen-$\gamma$ benchmark in Period~2.
    
    We compare THEMIS against four benchmarks: (i) \textbf{DELPHI-constant}, which freezes $\gamma$ at its training-period endpoint value throughout evaluation (i.e., no policy adjustment); (ii) \textbf{DELPHI-lookahead}, a non-causal oracle that re-fits DELPHI on both training and evaluation periods jointly, serving as an upper bound on achievable accuracy; and (iii) \textbf{SIR} and \textbf{SEIRD}, standard compartmental models (susceptible--infected--recovered, and susceptible--exposed--infected--recovered--deceased, respectively) without any policy-adjustment mechanism. The SIR and SEIRD baselines are included to demonstrate that simple compartmental models cannot match THEMIS's accuracy. All DELPHI-family models share the same underlying dynamics; the only difference is how $\gamma(t)$ is specified during the evaluation period. We report the mean absolute percentage error (APE) of daily cumulative case predictions over the evaluation window. We use simulated annealing for global optimization to ensure robust parameter estimates across all models.

This makes the policy message more concrete: lockdown is most useful as an early preemptive action, while exit depends on active hospitalization and case pressure rather than cumulative burden alone.

\textbf{(B) Online THEMIS.} At each decision date $\tau_s\in\{$Mar~15, Apr~15, May~15$\}$, the planner has already implemented the prefix $\bm{P}_{R,1:s-1}^{\mathrm{on}}$. The DELPHI parameters for region $R$ are given by the archived estimate $\hat{\theta}_R^{(s)}$ fitted on data available up to $\tau_s$. The policy-effect matrix is re-estimated on the corresponding date-restricted region--policy panel, yielding $\hat{\Gamma}^{(s)}$; for $s=1$, when the observed policy matrix is too sparse, $\hat{\Gamma}^{(1)}$ denotes the warm-start estimate described in the manuscript.

Given these inputs, the planner evaluates each candidate intervention $i\in\mathcal{I}$ by forming the provisional policy
\[
\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{R,s}(i)
=
\left(i_{R,1}^{\mathrm{on}},\ldots,i_{R,s-1}^{\mathrm{on}},i,\ldots,i\right),
\]
where the candidate $i$ appears in the current period and all remaining periods. The associated objective is
\[
c_{R,s}^{\mathrm{on}}(i)
=
c_R\!\left(\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{R,s}(i); \hat{\theta}_R^{(s)}, \hat{\Gamma}^{(s)}\right).
\]
The planner selects $i_{R,s}^{\mathrm{on}}\in\arg\min_{i\in\mathcal{I}}c_{R,s}^{\mathrm{on}}(i)$, implements it for one period, updates the data, and repeats. This yields the online policy $\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{on}}$. We compare $\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{on}}$ with the realized historical policy $\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{act}}$ and the hindsight optimum $\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{HS}}$.

\textbf{(C) Revealed-preference analysis.} We also use the realized policies to summarize heterogeneity in the economic--humanitarian trade-offs implicit in first-wave responses. For any policy $\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}$ and weight $w\in[0,1]$, define
\[
J_R(\bm{P};w)
=
w C_R^{\mathrm{hum}}(\bm{P})
+(1-w)C_R^{\mathrm{eco}}(\bm{P}).
\]
Let $\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{act}}$ denote the realized policy in region $R$. The revealed-preference weight is
\[
w_R^\star
\in
\arg\min_{w\in[0,1]}
\frac{J_R(\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{act}};w)}
{\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}} J_R(\bm{P};w)}.
\]
Thus, $w_R^\star$ is the humanitarian weight under which the realized policy is closest to the ex post efficient frontier, measured as a regret ratio. This statistic is descriptive. It does not estimate the policymaker's objective directly, but it provides a compact way to locate the realized policy on the model-implied trade-off.

Thank you for raising this point. We agree that, in a setting with multiple overlapping non-pharmaceutical interventions, it is important to consider the interaction effects between different policies. We would like to clarify that the THEMIS framework does \emph{not} assume that the effects of individual NPIs enter additively. Rather, each policy $i \in \mathcal{I}$ is defined as a mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive intervention bundle, and the corresponding quantity $\gamma_{R,i}$ captures the joint effect of that entire bundle in region $R$.

Specifically, the model is not written at the level of separate school-closure, gathering-restriction, and travel-restriction coefficients that are then summed. Following the advice of the AE, we conduct an out-of-sample time-transfer validation: we train the DELPHI model and estimate all $\gamma_{R,i}$ on one period, then evaluate prediction accuracy in a \emph{subsequent, non-overlapping} period using only the policy-driven counterfactual curve for $\gamma$.
    
\textbf{Experimental design.} We train DELPHI on March 15--June 15, 2020 (Period~1) and evaluate predictions on June 15--September 15, 2020 (Period~2). For the evaluation period, we construct the THEMIS counterfactual $\gamma$ curve using the \emph{actual} policies implemented in each region during Period~2, combined with the $\gamma_{R,i}$ estimates from Period~1. Specifically, we define $\gamma_R^P(t)$ as a piecewise constant function of the region-specific reduction factors:
\begin{equation}
	\gamma_R^P(t)=\begin{cases}
		\gamma_{R,i_0} & t_0\leq t<t_1\\
		\gamma_{R,i_1} & t_1\leq t<t_2\\ 
		\vdots & \vdots \\
		\gamma_{R,i_{k-1}} & t_{k-1}\leq t<t_k\\ 
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $i_j$ is the NPI in effect in Region $R$ during time window $[t_j, t_{j+1})$ in Period~2. The question is whether this piecewise curve, constructed entirely from Period~1 estimates, retains enough incremental signal to improve, or at least not materially worsen, predictions relative to a frozen-$\gamma$ benchmark in Period~2.

We compare THEMIS against four benchmarks: (i) \textbf{DELPHI-constant}, which freezes $\gamma$ at its training-period endpoint value throughout evaluation (i.e., no policy adjustment); (ii) \textbf{DELPHI-lookahead}, a non-causal oracle that re-fits DELPHI on both training and evaluation periods jointly, serving as an upper bound on achievable accuracy; and (iii) \textbf{SIR} and \textbf{SEIRD}, standard compartmental models (susceptible--infected--recovered, and susceptible--exposed--infected--recovered--deceased, respectively) without any policy-adjustment mechanism. The SIR and SEIRD baselines are included to demonstrate that simple compartmental models cannot match THEMIS's accuracy. All DELPHI-family models share the same underlying dynamics; the only difference is how $\gamma(t)$ is specified during the evaluation period. We report the mean absolute percentage error (APE) of daily cumulative case predictions over the evaluation window. We use simulated annealing for global optimization to ensure robust parameter estimates across all models.

In addition, we provide a detailed sensitivity analysis to show that our conclusions are robust to any unobserved confounding. Specifically, following the logic of \citet{cinelli2020making}, we design a sensitivity analysis for the relative ordering of candidate policies. For each region $R$, we shrink the estimated policy-specific effects $\gamma_{R,i}$ toward their cross-policy mean
\[
\bar{\gamma}_R = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}} \gamma_{R,i}
\]
by a fraction $f \in [0,1]$, so that
\[
\gamma_{R,i}^{(f)} = (1-f)\,\gamma_{R,i} + f\,\bar{\gamma}_R.
\]
At $f=0$, we recover the original estimates. At $f=1$, all policies are assigned the same $\gamma$ within a region, so NPIs no longer have any differential effect. This perturbation therefore provides a transparent way to model the possibility that part of the observed policy contrast is driven by unmeasured confounding rather than true policy differences.

For each value of $f$, we re-simulate all $7^3 = 343$ hypothetical policy sequences through the full THEMIS pipeline and record the resulting cost ranking. We then compare the perturbed ranking to the baseline ranking at $f=0$ using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient $\rho$ and record the first perturbation level at which the cost-minimizing policy changes. This construction focuses on the \emph{contrast} between policies, which is the quantity that drives the cost comparison in THEMIS, rather than the absolute level of $\gamma$, which is more directly affected by the overall transmission environment in a region.

Thank you for these two important and inter-related comments. They sit at the heart of how the paper should be read, and we agree that the original manuscript did not adequately address either question. We have substantially revised the paper in response, and outline below the four ways in which we believe the revision now addresses both concerns.

\textbf{1. Online THEMIS as a sequential decision procedure that can be deployed once early-wave data begin to accumulate.}

Your first concern asks directly: \emph{``could THEMIS be adapted to generate prospective forecasts mid-pandemic?''} We agree that the original submission did not establish this, and have therefore added Online THEMIS, a sequential one-step-look-ahead version of the framework that uses dated information at each decision point, together with a warm-start approximation in the first month when the data window is too short for the date-restricted ALS estimator. The full development is given in our response to AE Comment 5 (item B, ``Online THEMIS''); we summarize the key elements here.

At each decision date $\tau_s\in\{$Mar~15, Apr~15, May~15$\}$, the advisor estimates the DELPHI parameters $\hat{\theta}_R^{(s)}$ using only data available up to $\tau_s$ and re-estimates the policy-effect matrix $\hat{\Gamma}^{(s)}$ from the corresponding date-restricted region--policy panel when enough support is available. At the first decision date, when the observed matrix is too sparse, the procedure uses the baseline rank-$1$ estimate as a warm start. Given the implemented prefix $\bm{P}_{R,1:s-1}^{\mathrm{on}}$, the advisor evaluates each candidate intervention $i$ by forming $\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{R,s}(i)$, which appends $i$ to the current prefix and uses $i$ for the remaining periods. It then minimizes
\[
c_{R,s}^{\mathrm{on}}(i)
=
c_R\!\left(\widetilde{\bm{P}}_{R,s}(i); \hat{\theta}_R^{(s)}, \hat{\Gamma}^{(s)}\right).
\]
This constructs the three-month online policy $\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{on}}$ one decision at a time. We compare it to the realized historical policy $\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{act}}$ and to the unattainable hindsight-optimal sequence $\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{HS}}$ in Table~\ref{tab:rolling_advisor_v2}. For completeness, Figure~\ref{fig:rolling_advisor_v2} reproduces the same comparison in the response, although we omit the figure from the main text for brevity.

The results are economically meaningful. In Brazil, Spain, and New York, Online THEMIS materially improves on the realized policy: the gap from hindsight falls by $141.8$, $120.5$, and $117.3$ percentage points respectively. On average, the realized policy was $124.4\%$ above the hindsight optimum, while Online THEMIS was $39.5\%$ above it. We interpret this as evidence that, once limited early-wave data begin to accumulate, the framework can be used in a sequential decision-support mode rather than only for ex post evaluation.

Separately, we added a revealed-preference analysis to interpret the realized policies. This analysis does not evaluate the online advisor. Instead, it uses the realized policy $\bm{P}_R^{\mathrm{act}}$ to locate each region on the model-implied economic--humanitarian trade-off, through the descriptive weight $w_R^\star$ reported in Table~\ref{tab:revealed_preference_weights}. This supports the main-text discussion that Brazil's realized policy sits closer to the economic-cost frontier, while Germany's realized policy reflects a stronger humanitarian orientation.

\textbf{2. A region-blind state-action decision tree that operationalizes the framework's recommendations.}

Your first concern also asks: \emph{``If not, how would governments use the framework to inform decision-making?''} To address this, we have added a region-blind state-action decision tree (described in detail in our response to AE Comment 5, item A; Figure~\ref{fig:state_action_pooled_tree}). The tree maps the observable pandemic state at the start of each month, together with the policymaker's humanitarian-vs-economic weight $w$, to the cost-optimal next-month NPI. Crucially, the state vector deliberately excludes any region indicator or region-specific covariate (population, GDP per capita), so the tree must learn rules that depend only on observable pandemic and policy history. We view the resulting tree as an interpretable compression of the simulated policy map for the focal regions, rather than as a separately validated predictor for arbitrary new regions.

Pooling the counterfactual state-action pairs across the four focal regions, a depth-$4$ CART tree achieves five-fold cross-validation accuracy $0.47$, well above the random benchmark of $1/7 \approx 0.14$. Four substantive insights follow that go beyond ``early moderate restrictions'':

\begin{enumerate}
\item Increasing $w$ generally leads to more restrictive actions, but only conditional on the state. This is visible in comparisons such as L1 versus L2 and L8 versus L9 and L10.
\item Even economically oriented policymakers can choose restrictive policies. In leaves L3 and L4, when $0.03 \leq w \leq 0.34$, the recommended actions are NPI~6 and NPI~5 because current infections can generate later economic losses.
\item Exit from restrictions depends on active hospital pressure. In leaf L8, when cumulative economic cost exceeds $16.9\%$ of GDP and active hospitalizations are low, the tree recommends a less restrictive policy for all $w\leq0.92$.
\item Lockdown is selected only in leaf L6, when $w\geq0.34$, cumulative economic cost is at most $6.6\%$ of GDP, and active cases are at least $0.016\%$ of population. In the subtree where economic cost exceeds $16.9\%$ of GDP, lockdown is never optimal.
\end{enumerate}

Together with Online THEMIS, the tree delivers two complementary outputs: a policy-by-policy procedure for a planner who can run the simulator sequentially, and a compact summary of the simulated policy map in observable per-capita and per-GDP units.

\textbf{3. The methodological contribution: a rank-$1$ framework for partial counterfactual matrices.}

We agree that the original manuscript did not articulate the methodological contribution clearly enough, and we have revised the paper to make that contribution explicit. The central methodological step underlying every counterfactual exercise is the joint estimation of policy and region effects under a rank-$1$ structure on the partially observed region--policy effect matrix $\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{R}|\times|\mathcal{I}|}$. We summarize that revision here.

The fundamental statistical problem THEMIS solves is one of partial-treatment counterfactual estimation: each region is observed under only a subset of the seven NPI categories ($24.9\%$ of $\Gamma$ entries are observed in Period~1, $19.9\%$ in Period~2, and $20.0\%$ in Period~3), and the counterfactual exercise requires reconstructing $\Gamma$ on its missing entries. The revised manuscript now positions this estimation problem squarely within the established low-rank counterfactual literature in econometrics and statistics. Specifically, \citet{athey2021matrix} use matrix completion methods to estimate missing potential outcomes in panel data with partial treatment support; \citet{bai2009panel} develops panel-data models with interactive fixed effects, which are a special case of a low-rank factor structure on a partially observed outcome matrix; and \citet{moon2015linear} study linear regression models with low-rank cross-sectional dependence. Our specification $\gamma_{R,i}=k_R\gamma_i$ is the rank-$1$ member of this class, with both $k_R$ and $\gamma_i$ estimated jointly from the data via non-negative alternating least squares. We believe this framing helps clarify the methodological core of the paper.

The empirical case for the rank-$1$ specification is now much stronger (full development in our response to AE Comment 1.1). Three pieces of evidence support it: (i) higher-rank specifications do not improve out-of-sample fit (Table~\ref{tab:low_rank}); (ii) the rank-$1$ model improves on simpler policy-only and region-only benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:alt_specs}); and (iii) the imputed counterfactual entries are highly correlated across rank choices (Figure~\ref{fig:rank1_vs_rank3_scatter}). Beyond COVID-19, the broader methodological lesson is that pandemic policy data exhibit the partial-support structure for which low-rank regularization is particularly natural: many regions, many candidate interventions, but only a few interventions per region.

\textbf{4. On the appropriate standard of contribution for this paper.}

Finally, we respectfully draw attention to the framing offered by both the Department Editor and the Associate Editor. The Department Editor wrote: \emph{``I am ok sacrificing `rigor' or theoretical contributions for impact, especially on this problem.''} The Associate Editor similarly observed: \emph{``This work clearly doesn't fit conventional categories. While the authors acknowledge methodological limitations in rigor, their effort in analyzing extensive real-world data is commendable. The potential policy impact appears significant, especially considering the underlying DELPHI forecasts have already been incorporated into CDC and other organizational forecasting models.''} 

We share the view that the right standard for this paper is its applied and empirical contribution rather than the derivation of a new structural theorem about optimal control of epidemics. There is a long-standing tradition in MSOM and the broader operations management literature of impact-driven applied work whose contribution lies not in a single new analytical result but in the careful integration, calibration, and deployment of a decision framework on a problem of major societal importance. THEMIS sits squarely in this tradition. Concretely, the contributions of the revised paper are: (i) a cross-region, cost-internalizing counterfactual evaluation of the full $7^3$ space of three-month NPI sequences, calibrated on data from $210$ regions and four focal countries; (ii) the explicit framing of the policy-effect imputation problem as a partially observed low-rank matrix problem, with the empirical validation of the rank-$1$ structure described above and in the new Appendix~\ref*{ms-app:separability}; (iii) Online THEMIS, which demonstrates how the framework can be used sequentially once early-wave data begin to accumulate; (iv) a region-blind state-action tree that compresses the simulation output into monitorable thresholds stated in per-capita and per-GDP units; and (v) a fully open-source codebase that makes the framework reusable for other regions.

To answer your final question directly, \emph{``is there a novel insight about trade-offs, timing, or regional heterogeneity that generalizes across contexts?''}, we believe the answer is yes, but in a deliberately qualified sense. At the policy-rule level, the decision tree highlights a set of recurring patterns within the four focal regions: active rather than cumulative burden drives the key splits, exit is tied to hospital pressure and accumulated economic cost, and lockdown appears only as an early preemptive tool. At the framework level, the paper shows that a parsimonious rank-$1$ counterfactual structure can organize a large sparse policy matrix and deliver stable qualitative conclusions under a broad set of robustness checks. Neither point is a universal law of pandemic control, but both move the paper beyond a purely descriptive SEIR-style comparison.

We hope these four developments, Online THEMIS, the decision tree, the explicit connection to the low-rank counterfactual literature, and the clarified framing of contribution, collectively address both halves of your comment and demonstrate that the framework provides a meaningful form of decision support rather than only a retrospective evaluation exercise.

To address this concern, we have conducted a sensitivity analysis in which we replace the linear mapping from NPI stringency to depression prevalence with two deliberately extreme nonlinear alternatives that bracket the linear model from both sides:

\begin{itemize}
